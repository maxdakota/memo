Rendering Minds

We see a future where billions of sub-agents will participate in the global economy as both producers and buyers, exchanging trillions of dollars in value. Today, most economic activity occurs between firms and their clients, vendors, partners, and regulators. As intelligence arbitrage accelerates, the humans mediating these relationships will increasingly be augmented, or replaced, by agents acting on their behalf.

When this happens, the bottleneck on economic activity will not be model capability or compute. It will be coordination. How will agents work together in cooperative and adversarial contexts? How will they share knowledge, resolve disputes, and delegate authority across organizations? These are open questions.

Rendering Minds is working to answer them.

01

Why the Existing Approach Failed

The path to agentic organizations runs through data. The same data that teaches frontier models how to perform economically valuable work also reveals what infrastructure is required for scalable, multi-agent coordination.

In 2012, crowdsourced annotation worked because tasks were simple, workers were easy to source, and data easy to QA. Tasks grew harder but remained tractable. Today, frontier data types — long-horizon work, multi-agent coordination, multi-turn model behavior — present new challenges. These cannot be captured through conventional annotation interfaces. They require ground-truth signal from operational environments.

The leading vendors are now partnering with established companies to document and instrument their workflows. This runs into institutional inertia, third-party consent concerns, and procurement cycles that move too slowly. Neither approach is producing the data the next generation of AI requires.

02

Our Approach: ACEs

An ACE (Autonomous Commercial Entity) is a semi-autonomous firm that serves paying clients. ACEs earn revenue, manage P&L, and operate under market pressures.

Each ACE is anchored by a Director: a human domain expert who decomposes the service into roles, specifies what each role requires, defines what good looks like at each stage, and makes the judgment calls that agents cannot yet make.

Over time, as agents improve and operating procedures accumulate, the Director's role narrows and the ACE becomes more autonomous.

The domains that fit the ACE model best share three properties:

  • The work is bottlenecked by expensive human labor.
  • The service can be decomposed into discrete, assignable tasks.
  • There is a clear signal that tells you whether the output was good.

Examples include a software development agency, financial research firm, and regulatory compliance service. In each case, the Director brings the expertise to decompose the work and review the output, and the agents perform the roles in between.

ACEs serve two purposes:

  • Data. Each ACE produces frontier training data that cannot be generated through conventional annotation: long-horizon agent trajectories, multi-agent coordination traces, escalation and recovery patterns. This data is sold to frontier labs.
  • Platform. Each ACE is a live experiment that builds and validates the coordination layer needed to govern agents doing this work. This produces a growing library of firm structures, agent personas, and operating principles.
03

First ACE: Autonomous Data Factory

Frontier AI labs need industrial volumes of high-quality data. Today, the collection process is manual and operationally intensive. It requires teams of project managers, auditors, data scientists, and other supporting staff. Our first ACE, an Autonomous Data Factory, replaces the human data operations layer with an agentic workforce. Agents source and vet domain experts, track their performance on projects, conduct quality control, own the project's P&L, and deliver data directly to clients.

We start here for three reasons.

  • We have run these pipelines at scale. We are the first Directors, instantiating our own domain expertise in an agentic workforce.
  • Data projects are high-velocity, which gives us the fastest feedback loops to iterate on and improve the coordination layer through use.
  • Frontier labs and human data vendors have an acute need for specific data types that we will produce. Remaining at the frontier is the fastest path to authority in the market that follows.

Where This Goes

Near term
ACEs

Serving frontier labs on the highest-value, hardest-to-source data types: multi-agent coordination, user simulation, ultra-long-horizon code.

Medium term
Multi-Agent Coordination for Enterprises

As enterprise use cases catch up to model capabilities, we will be positioned to provide coordination infrastructure, having spent years deploying agents in semi-autonomous ACEs across many domains.

Long term
Infrastructure for the Multi-Agent Economy

The protocols, knowledge-sharing systems, and coordination mechanisms that let billions of agents transact within and across organizations.
