Rendering Minds

We see a future where billions of sub-agents will participate in the global economy as both producers and buyers, exchanging trillions of dollars in value. Today, most economic activity occurs between firms and their clients, vendors, partners, and regulators. As intelligence arbitrage accelerates, the humans mediating these relationships will increasingly be augmented, or replaced, by agents acting on their behalf.

When this happens, the bottleneck on economic activity will not be model capability or compute. It will be coordination. How will agents work together in cooperative and adversarial contexts? How will they share knowledge, resolve disputes, and delegate authority across organizations? These are open questions.

Rendering Minds is working to answer them.

01

Why the Existing Approach Failed

The path to agentic organizations runs through data. The same data that teaches frontier models how to perform economically valuable work also reveals what infrastructure is required for scalable, multi-agent coordination.

In 2012, crowdsourced annotation worked because tasks were simple, workers were easy to source, and data easy to QA. Tasks grew harder but remained tractable. Today, frontier data types — long-horizon work, multi-agent coordination, multi-turn model behavior — present new challenges. These cannot be captured through conventional annotation interfaces. They require ground-truth signal from the real world.

The leading vendors are now partnering with established companies to document and instrument their workflows. This runs into institutional inertia, third-party consent concerns, and procurement cycles that move too slowly. Neither approach is producing the data the next generation of AI requires.

02

Our Approach: Pods

We are building Pods: semi-autonomous firms that perform services for real clients. Each Pod is anchored by a Pod Lead, a human domain expert who decomposes the service into jobs to be done, specifies the tools and context required, and oversees a fleet of agents hired to fill the resulting organizational structure.

The domains that fit the Pod model best share three properties:

  • The work is currently bottlenecked by expensive human labor.
  • The jobs within the service can be decomposed into discrete, assignable tasks.
  • There is a clear quality signal that tells you whether the output was good.

Legal due diligence run by an M&A lawyer, clinical trial operations run by a clinical director, insurance processing run by a senior adjuster. In each case, the Pod Lead brings the expertise to decompose the service and review the output, and the agents perform the roles in between.

The Pod structure accomplishes two things simultaneously. It generates frontier data that can be sold to labs as RL environments and long-horizon training data. More importantly, it builds and validates the coordination layer needed to govern agents doing real work. This produces a growing library of firm structures, agent personas, and operating principles.

03

First Pod: Semi-Autonomous Pods

Frontier AI labs need industrial volumes of high-quality data. Today, the collection process is manual and operationally intensive, requiring large teams of project managers. Semi-Autonomous Pods replaces the operations layer with an agentic workforce. Agents source, vet, and train domain experts, measure their performance, ensure quality control, and package data for delivery to clients.

We start here for three reasons.

  • We have run these pipelines at scale. We are the first Pod Leads, instantiating our own domain expertise in an agentic workforce.
  • Data projects are high-velocity: they give us the fastest feedback loops to iterate on and improve the coordination layer through real use.
  • Frontier labs and leading data vendors have high standards and an acute need for the specific data types that are hardest to produce. Remaining at the frontier is the fastest path to authority in the market that follows.
